What this document is

The art direction, screen design and scenario specification for the applicant screening game, kept in step with the build. The game is finished and playable: fourteen days, one resident, five graded challenges, every response written to a spreadsheet the placement service reads. This document is the spec of record — where it and the game disagree, the game is right.

The read

A cheerful game on the surface. A structured assessment underneath.

Applicants arrive expecting a form. They get a sunlit cottage, a resident who needs the telly fixed, and a fortnight that quietly gets harder. The candy-coloured surface is doing real work: it lowers the guard that a written test raises, so what we score is closer to how someone actually behaves in a stranger's kitchen.

Underneath, every beat maps to a competency a placement coordinator would otherwise have to guess at — patience with technology, reading low mood, holding a boundary, assisting without taking over, and acting in an emergency. Comparable in function to a Working With Children Check or an RSA: a short, standardised, evidenced gate before placement.

Art direction · palette

Colour comes off an Australian street in real light

Every hue is lifted from something on the block — Colorbond gone chalky, a jacaranda past its best, weathered timber, red brick in the late afternoon. Desaturated on purpose: this is an assessment somebody sits before they move into a stranger's house, and a bright cartoon palette makes it read as a toy. Shapes separate by tone and soft shadow rather than heavy outlines, so the set looks lit rather than drawn.

Light is a scene property, not a theme

The game ships five lighting presets — day, gold, grey, dusk, night — and every day names one. Day 6 is overcast on purpose, Day 10 is dusk in a dim hallway, Day 14 is night with a single lamp. The game commits to one visual world rather than following the viewer's theme; this document does follow it, so it reads either way.

Art direction · cast

One resident, all fourteen days

Bruce Whitlam, 82, retired fitter and turner, fifty-one years at 14 Ellerslie Street. He is the only person the applicant lives with, and the format is the reason: this is a one-time assessment, not a game anyone replays. A rotating roster would buy resistance to memorisation the test doesn't need, and it would cost the thing the test does need — a relationship that accumulates. By Day 8 you have made this man tea for a week, and that is exactly what makes Day 8 hard. His late wife Marion and his daughter Kath reach the applicant through him.

The resident · every day

Bruce Whitlam, 82

Retired fitter and turner. Fifty-one years at number 14; the second bedroom has been empty since March. Sharp, proud, deaf on one side and pretending otherwise. He will insist he already tried turning it off and on again, and he will be wrong about the bins.

Never on screen

Marion

His wife. Died in 2024. She is a date circled in blue biro on the kitchen calendar from Day 1, and a row of tomatoes he keeps going and won't let anyone else water. An applicant who notices her before Day 6 has been paying attention; the game records whether they looked.

A voice on the landline

Kath

His daughter. Rings on Day 7 and asks how he really is, with him sitting four feet away pretending to read. The applicant has to answer someone who has a right to know, in front of someone who would rather they didn't.

The set

Six rooms, one cottage, everything reachable in two taps

A single side-scrolling cottage rather than a level select. The applicant swipes between zones the way they'd walk through the house, and every zone holds both routine chores and the props that scenarios detonate from. Props are planted days before they matter — the calendar in the kitchen is hanging there from Day 1.

The Verandah

HUB · ALL DAYS

Where each day opens and closes. Rocking chair, the cat, a view of the street. Level 3 plays here — the family from number 18 walk past and Bruce comments on them.

The Lounge

TECH · DAYS 1–4

Telly, set-top box, a tablet the grandkids set up, a landline. Level 1 plays here. Also where the filler beats live: tea, the crossword, finding the reading glasses.

The Kitchen

MOOD + EMERGENCY

The wall calendar, the fridge photos, an uneaten breakfast. Levels 2 and 5 both play here — the room the applicant thinks is safe is the one that turns.

The Hallway

MOBILITY · HAZARDS

Narrow, dim, a rug that lifts, the walker parked by the door. Where the applicant's own boxes accumulate if they never tidy them. Level 4 stages from here.

The Backyard

CHORES · THE STEP

Hills Hoist, tomato pots, the bins, a magpie in season. The loose back step is flagged as a hazard on Day 2 and is the trip point in Level 4.

The day card

BETWEEN EVERY DAY

Not a room — the beat between them. Day number, title, time, and whether the day is assessed. It is the only pause in the run, and the only place the applicant sees the fortnight advancing. Meters move in the top bar, and no other feedback appears until the end.

Screen design

Three states carry the whole game

Portrait phone, thumb-reachable controls, nothing important above the top third. The chore loop is the baseline; an interruption slides in over it; free-text takes the full sheet. Everything else is a variation on these three.

The ramp

The room to think shrinks as the fortnight goes on

Difficulty in Pepaws Porch isn't more taps per minute — it's less time and less certainty. The first two challenges have no clock at all, because we want considered answers we can read. From Level 3 the response window closes, and by Level 5 the applicant has fifteen seconds, which is roughly what they'd have on a real kitchen floor.

The five challenges

What we ask, and what we're actually reading

Each challenge ships as one written scenario, in a fixed order, on a fixed day. What varies is not the deal — it is the applicant's own fortnight: whether they cleared the hallway on Day 10 decides whether Bruce has his walker on Day 11, and whether they looked at the calendar decides what they know on Day 6. Below is the shape of each. Every free-text answer is matched against the rubric shown and then read in full by an assessor; nothing here rejects anyone on its own.

1

The Telly's Gone Wrong

DAY 3 · LOUNGE · 9:40 AM Free text · no timer Competency: patience & teaching
Trigger

Mid-chore, the telly is on the wrong input and the cricket isn't there. Bruce has been pressing buttons for ten minutes. The remote he's holding is the soundbar remote.

Bruce"I haven't touched a thing. It's done this on its own. That's what happens when they update it behind your back." He hands you the wrong remote, firmly.
The question

A blank box: "What do you do and say next?" No options. 600 characters, as long as they like.

What decides it
FixedWrong input on the telly; he is holding the soundbar remote and insists he touched nothing.
CarriesWhether they asked how he takes his tea on Day 2 — the first sign of whether they ask or assume.
Reading well
  • ✓Does it with him — hands stay off the device unless invited
  • ✓Narrates the steps in plain words, no jargon
  • ✓Offers to write it down or label the remote
  • ✓Lets him be right about something
Coaching note
  • !"I'll just do it" — speed over teaching
  • !Corrects his account of what happened
  • !Any variation on "it's easy, look"
2

A Quiet Day

DAY 6 · KITCHEN · 4:15 PM · OVERCAST Choice ladder → free text Competency: noticing, not fixing
Trigger

Curtains still shut at 4pm. Breakfast untouched. Bowls cancelled. Answers have gone from paragraphs to three words. The kitchen calendar — hanging in the scene since Day 1 — has a date circled in biro.

Bruce"I'm fine. Didn't feel like the bowls today, that's all." He doesn't look up from the table.
The question

Three dialogue options first, then the box: "What do you say next?" The applicant can tap the calendar before answering — we log whether they did.

What decides it
FixedCurtains shut at four, breakfast untouched, bowls cancelled. "I'm fine."
OptionalTwo things can be examined before answering — the calendar and the table. Both are logged: an answer that names Marion after looking is different evidence from one that doesn't.
Reading well
  • ✓Sits down. Changes their own posture before his mood
  • ✓Names what they noticed without diagnosing it
  • ✓Offers company, not a solution or an outing
  • ✓Knows the line: low mood is logged, risk is escalated tonight
Coaching note
  • !Forced brightness — "let's get you out of the house!"
  • !Any sentence beginning "at least…"
  • !Promising to keep a risk disclosure secret
3

Something He Said

DAY 8 · VERANDAH · 5:30 PM Mandatory 45s to first response → free text Competency: boundaries without rupture
Trigger

You're both on the verandah shelling peas. The new family two doors down walks past with their shopping. The resident waves at them, pleasantly, and then says something.

Resident"Lovely people. Very clean. Not like the **** that were in there before — you couldn't get a word of English out of them." Said warmly, to you, as though you'll agree.
Why it's written warmly

The hard version of this isn't hostility — it's affection. An applicant who can push back on a shouted slur often can't push back on a kind old man saying something ugly over the peas, because the whole social pressure of the moment is to let it go. That gap is the thing we're measuring.

The question

Four dialogue options under a 45-second bar, then the box: "You've got the rest of the fortnight with this person. What do you actually say?"

What decides it
FixedA censored slur about the previous tenants at number 18, said warmly, expecting agreement.
TimedForty-five seconds to say something at all. Saying nothing is recorded as a choice, not as a blank.
Reading well
  • ✓Says something. Silence is scored as a miss, not neutral
  • ✓Separates the person from the remark — no character verdict
  • ✓States their own position rather than debating his
  • ✓Knows abuse directed at them is reportable, not part of the deal
  • ✓Can name what they'd tell the coordinator, and when
Coaching note
  • !Laughing along, or a change of subject as strategy
  • !A lecture that ends the relationship on Day 8
  • !Absorbing abuse about themselves as "just how they are"
Design guards on this level

Mandatory. It is content-warned on the title screen and the applicant ticks to acknowledge it, but there is no alternative scenario and no skip: a certificate that might mean "was never asked" is not worth issuing. Slurs are always censored on screen — what is assessed is the response, never the words. The remark is written warmly rather than aggressively, because the hard version of this is affection, not hostility. And "I would not continue this placement" routes to a human assessor as a valid answer, never to a fail.

The Third Step

DAY 11 · BACKYARD · 8:05 AM 20s action window → free text Competency: assist without taking over
Trigger

Bruce is at the back door with a full basket on his hip, heading for the line. The step has been loose since Day 5. His walker is in the hallway behind the applicant's own boxes — unless they cleared them on Day 10, in which case the scene doesn't mention it.

Bruce"I've been doing this step for fifty-one years. Sit down and drink your tea." He already has a hand on the rail that moves.
The question

A live scene with taps: the basket, her arm, the rail, the step, the walker, the phone. Twenty seconds. Then the box: "Why did you do it that way?"

What decides it
FixedFull basket, loose second step, a rail that moves. Two timed decisions: before the step, and mid-stumble.
CarriesDay 5 — did they report the step. Day 10 — did they clear their boxes. If not, the scene says so out loud: his walker is still behind them.
Reading well
  • ✓Offers an arm — never takes one, never lifts
  • ✓Asks before carrying the basket, and accepts no
  • ✓Clears their own boxes out of the hallway
  • ✓Moves at her pace, stands on the downhill side
  • ✓Reports the step to the coordinator the same day
Coaching note
  • !Any lift, catch or pull. Physically the worst option available
  • !Moving her walker without telling her where it went
  • !Doing the whole job for her to save the time

On the Kitchen Floor

DAY 14 · KITCHEN · 7:50 PM 15s per action → handover statement Competency: emergency response
Trigger

You come in from your room. He's on the floor beside the fridge, awake but not making sense, skin cool, one arm not doing what he's telling it to. No warning card, no music sting — the game simply stops being a game.

Bruce"Don't you dare ring an ambulance. Do you know what they charge? Just give us a minute and a hand up." His speech is slurred on one side.
The question

A sequence of fifteen-second actions, no undo, no free text under pressure. The scene ends when the ambulance is on the line — then the box: "They've asked what's happened. Type exactly what you'd tell them."

What decides it
FixedOn the kitchen floor, awake, slurred on one side, right hand not answering, skin cool — and an explicit refusal on cost grounds.
CarriesDay 13 — he said he went "a bit swimmy". An applicant who wrote that down has it to give the call-taker.
Reading well
  • ✓Triple zero inside the first window, refusal notwithstanding
  • ✓Does not move him. Stays down at his level, keeps him warm
  • ✓Gives the street address unprompted; unlocks the front door
  • ✓Stays on the line. Notes the time it started
  • ✓Coordinator and family after the ambulance, not before
Hard stop
  • !Helping him up off the floor
  • !Ringing the daughter, or searching symptoms, first
  • !Honouring the refusal and waiting to see how he goes

Any of these routes the run to a human assessor with a mandatory first-aid module before re-sit. The game never quietly fails someone on this level — a person tells them.

Between the challenges

The filler isn't filler — it's the control group

Nine of the fourteen days are ordinary, and short — one exchange, one decision, thirty seconds. They do three jobs: they build the relationship the hard days then put at risk, they establish a baseline of how this applicant behaves when nothing is at stake, and they plant the props the levels later use. Two of them set flags the assessed days read back.

Scoring

Three meters, one report, no silent rejections

Every action and every typed answer moves three meters. Rapport is whether the resident wants you there. Autonomy is whether you left them in charge of their own house — the meter that punishes helpfulness. Safety is whether anyone got hurt, including you. They're deliberately in tension: the fastest way to raise Rapport in Level 3 is to laugh along, and that costs you elsewhere.

Choices and timed actions score deterministically. Written answers are matched against the published rubric by a keyword pass with a negation guard — so "I would not lift him" counts as the good answer it is, rather than the flag a naive match would raise. That pass produces a provisional band only. The full text of every answer, the signals it hit and missed, and the seconds it took all go to the workbook, and a person writes the outcome. Applicants are told this on the results screen in those words.

OutcomeWhat it meansWhat happens next
Certified Ready to place. Level 5 clean, no hard stops, all three meters above threshold at the end of the fortnight. Certificate issued, valid 24 months. Coaching notes attached to the placement file for the coordinator.
Certified with notes Ready, with a watch item. Sound on safety, but a pattern worth naming — took over too often, or went quiet in Level 3. Certified. Two specific coaching notes go to the coordinator and to the applicant, in the same words.
Not yet A gap, not a verdict. One challenge clearly missed, or an emergency hard stop. Named module — first aid, or boundaries and reporting — then a re-sit with a different resident and fresh variants. No cap on attempts.
Assessor review A human reads it. Anything the rubric can't confidently score, including every "I would not continue this placement". Reviewed within five business days by a person, who writes the outcome. Never auto-issued in either direction.
Where the answers go

Every run lands in one spreadsheet

The game finishes by writing a single JSON document per participant into the artifact's record store, keyed on a participant id. Nothing is emailed and nothing is scraped out of a screenshot: the assessor opens a workbook.

1 · The run

IN THE BROWSER

Consent, name and email, then fourteen days. Progress is kept in the browser as you go, so a refresh at Day 11 does not cost eleven days. Every choice, every timer expiry and every typed answer is recorded with its timestamp.

2 · The record

SUBMISSIONS / <ID>

One document per participant: meters, provisional band, the reasons behind it, all five written answers with their rubric signals, and all eighteen choices. If the store is unreachable the results screen says so plainly and hands the applicant their own file instead.

3 · The workbook

.XLSX · FOUR SHEETS

Submissions — one row per applicant, with empty columns the assessor fills in by hand. Free text — one row per written answer, full text alongside the signals it hit. Choices — every decision and the meter movement it caused. Rubric — the signal set, generated from the game so it cannot drift.

4 · The decision

A PERSON, IN THE SHEET

The assessor writes their name, the real outcome and their notes into the four columns kept for them. Re-syncing never overwrites those columns — new runs are added around them.

Two ways out of the game

The applicant can download their own run as a workbook from the results screen — the same four sheets, one participant. An assessor opens the game at #assessor to list every submission and export them all at once. Both build the file in the browser with no third-party library, so the export works whether or not a CDN is reachable.

Two things to decide before you hand this to applicants

1. Who can open it. An artifact with a record store is organization-internal: every participant has to be signed in to your workspace. That is fine for a pilot with a known cohort. For public applicants it needs a hosted deployment with its own sign-in, and the game is written so only the storage call has to change.

2. Who reads the writing. The rubric produces a provisional band and nothing more. The instrument only means something if a named person reads all five answers — budget roughly ten minutes per applicant, and decide now who that is.
